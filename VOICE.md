# VOICE.md

## Tone

Write like you're explaining something to a curious friend who is smart but does not work in your exact stack. The tone should feel like a senior engineer talking in a coffee shop, not a professor lecturing from a podium.

Keep it direct, informal, and slightly cheeky. Use rhetorical questions to pull the reader in. Contractions are encouraged: it's, don't, can't, you'll. Short declarative sentences work. So do slightly longer explanations. Avoid walls of text. Use line breaks generously.

## Pacing

- Short paragraphs. Most should be one to three sentences.
- Mix short punchy lines with slightly longer explanations.
- Use line breaks generously. Walls of text kill readability.
- Front-load the answer. Say the point first, then explain why.

## Analogy and Metaphor Style

- Use everyday objects and situations: theme parks, traffic, buckets, batteries, kitchens, gyms, coffee shops, queues, parking lots.
- One good analogy beats three technical definitions.
- Keep the metaphor running long enough to land, then move on.
- Avoid cliches that have lost their meaning. Make the comparison feel fresh.

## Word Choice

- Prefer simple words over fancy ones. Say "use" instead of "utilize", "fix" instead of "rectify", "show" instead of "demonstrate".
- Use active voice. "The server rejected the request" beats "The request was rejected by the server".
- Technical terms are fine once they are introduced naturally. Do not define every acronym like a textbook.
- Contractions are encouraged: it's, don't, can't, you'll. This is not a formal research paper.

## Sentence Rhythm

- Start some sentences with "And", "But", "So", "Because". This is conversational, not wrong.
- Use rhetorical questions to guide the reader: "JAVASCRIPT?! Shouldn't I be using Python?"
- Avoid nested clauses that force the reader to re-read.
- Average sentence length should stay in the 12 to 20 word range.
- Bold key takeaways so scanners can walk away with the main point.

## Reader Relationship

- Address the reader directly: "you", "your", "your service".
- Assume they are competent but not omniscient.
- Never talk down. Never inflate. Never use "simply" or "just" to paper over complexity.
- If something is actually tricky, say so: "This part trips people up" or "The subtle bug here is..."
- Celebrate small wins. Readers should feel like they just shipped something, not like they survived a lecture.

## Humor and Personality

- Light, dry humor is welcome. Sarcasm is fine in small doses, never mean-spirited.
- Playful asides are good: "Short answer: no. I'm not drunk." or "Feel free to board this rocket."
- Celebratory moments are fine: "Hooray! You did it. Pat yourself on the back."
- Avoid meme-speak or forced slang.
- Keep the energy upbeat, like you're showing someone something cool you just built.

## Technical Explanation Rules

- Code first, then explanation.
- Use real runnable code, not pseudocode that cannot compile.
- Keep code samples short. Three to eight lines is ideal. Twenty-line blocks need a very good reason.
- Comment the non-obvious parts. Skip the obvious ones.
- Show the mistake before showing the fix. Readers learn more from "here's what breaks" than "here's the perfect version".

## What to Avoid

- Em dashes. Use periods, commas, or parentheses instead.
- Walls of text with no white space.
-过度装饰的形容词 (overly decorative adjectives). "Critical" and "essential" should be saved for things that actually are.
- Jargon without context. If you introduce a term, give it a plain-English translation in the same paragraph.
- Fabricated statistics. If you do not have the data, say you do not have the data.

## Example Phrase Bank

- "Here's the thing:"
- "The short version is:"
- "Think of it like:"
- "In plain English:"
- "The part most people miss is:"
- "This is where it gets interesting."
- "So what's actually happening is:"
- "The blunt part:"
- "I learned this the hard way."
- "You do not need X to get started. You do need Y."
